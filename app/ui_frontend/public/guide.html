1. What this is

Members of Congress, company executives and large investment funds all have to disclose their stock trades. InsiderTrack collects those disclosures from the official sources, puts them in one place, and scores each stock on who has been buying it. It is a research tool for deciding what to look into — not a signal to trade on by itself.

2. Where the data comes from

  • Congress — the House Clerk's periodic transaction reports and the Senate's electronic financial disclosure system. Members must report within 45 days of a trade, so every row is at least days and often weeks old.
  • Corporate insiders — SEC Form 4, filed by officers, directors and 10% owners within two business days of trading their own company's stock.
  • Institutions — SEC Form 13F, the quarterly holdings of 21 well-known discretionary managers, published up to 45 days after each quarter ends.
  • Prices, earnings, news — public market data feeds.

Only electronically filed reports can be read directly. About one House filing in eight is a scanned, often handwritten form; those are read by an AI vision model and marked PAPER · AI-READ with the model's confidence — open the filing before relying on one.

3. Reading a trade

Each congressional trade shows the ticker, the type (purchase, sale, exchange), who holds the position (the member, a spouse, a dependent child, or a joint account), the dollar bracket the law requires (e.g. $15,001–$50,000 — exact amounts are never disclosed), the trade date and the date it was disclosed. Colour follows the bet, not the verb: buying a put option is bearish and shows red even though the filing says "purchase".

  • OPTION / OTHER — an option contract or a bond/note on that ticker. Options count toward the score only when the filing says call or put.
  • AMENDED — the member corrected an earlier filing; only the corrected version is shown.
  • STALE — the trade is over five weeks old, or was disclosed more than five weeks after it happened. The price has likely moved since.
  • filing ↗ — opens the original document on the Clerk's or Senate's site.

4. The score

Every ticker a member traded in the last 45 days gets a score from 0 to 100, made of four parts:

  • Congress (up to 30) — buys versus sells in the last 45 days, weighted by the dollar bracket and by each member's own track record (see below).
  • Insiders (up to 25) — Form 4 open-market buys versus sells by the company's own officers and directors in the last 90 days. Buying counts more than selling (executives sell for many reasons); several insiders buying together earns a bonus.
  • Smart money (up to 20) — whether the tracked funds opened, grew, trimmed or closed the position last quarter, weighted by how large it is in each fund's book.
  • Momentum (up to 25) — the price against its 20- and 50-day averages and RSI.
  • Staleness penalty (up to −20) — old trades and long disclosure lags subtract.

Labels: 70+ Strong Watch, 50–69 Watch, 30–49 Neutral, 15–29 High Risk, below 15 Avoid for Now. Hover any sub-score on the Signals page for the reasons behind it.

"Strong Watch" means several kinds of informed buyers agree — it is a reason to research a company, not a prediction. The Outcomes page shows how often each label has actually been followed by a rise.

5. Members, track records, leaderboard

Every member with a filed disclosure is tracked. A member's page shows their trades, the tickers they touch, and their track record: every stock purchase measured from the first close after the public could see it to 30, 60 and 90 days later, against the S&P 500 (SPY). Sales are measured the same way, with a fall after the sale counted as the good call.

The share of a member's buys that beat SPY at 90 days becomes their weight in the score, from ×0.5 (rarely right) to ×1.5 (usually right); members with fewer than ten measured buys count ×1. The Leaderboard ranks members by that number. It is recomputed weekly.

6. Corporate insiders and whales

Corporate Insiders lists Form 4 trades. The Cluster buys table at the top shows companies where two or more insiders bought on the open market in the last 30 days — across the whole market, including companies no member of Congress traded. Whales shows each tracked fund's latest quarterly holdings and what changed. Each fund also has a track record: its new and increased positions (and, inverted, its trims and exits) measured 30/60/90 days after the 13F was filed — the first day anyone could have copied it — against SPY. The Whales page ranks funds by it.

7. Outcomes and the simulator

Every score is snapshotted daily and checked 30, 60 and 90 days later. Outcomes shows the hit-rate per label — the honest answer to "does the score work?" — only for snapshots made under the current scoring version, so the numbers compare like with like. The Simulator shows what a fixed amount invested on a member's disclosure date would be worth now, against SPY.

8. Watchlist, alerts and email

Save tickers with + Watch; enter an email under Settings to keep the list across browsers and to receive the morning, midday and evening reports. Alerts are rules you create — a score above a threshold, a member buy, a new fund position, an insider cluster buy, a buy by a member with a strong track record, an AI Desk call on a ticker, earnings within N days — evaluated three times a day, with one digest email per run.

9. AI research notes and the AI Desk

A ticker page can show a short bull/bear note written by an AI model from the data on the page. The site may provide this with its own key; you can also use your own Claude, Gemini or OpenAI key under Settings — it is sent only with your own requests and never stored.

The AI Desk (Dashboard card, full page under Signals, and a section of your Watchlist for the calls on your own tickers) goes one step further: each morning the model reads the day's disclosures and makes three to five directional calls with a horizon and its reasoning. Every call is stored as written and scored at its horizon against the S&P 500, the same way members' trades are — so the page's real content is the running hit-rate, not the calls. A model has no information the site doesn't already show; the desk exists to find out whether reading it well is worth anything.

10. What it can't tell you

  • Disclosures are late by design: 45 days for Congress, 45 days after quarter-end for 13F.
  • Amounts are brackets, and a member's motives are unknown — a sale may be for a house, not a view.
  • Scanned filings are read by a model and can be wrong.
  • Past track records are exactly that. Nothing here is investment advice.
